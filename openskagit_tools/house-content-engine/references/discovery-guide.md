# Homeowner Content Discovery Guide

## Mission

Discover homeowner questions that can become evidence-backed investigations rather than generic real-estate explanations.

Preferred pattern:

**homeowner concern → unanswered question → hypothesis → evidence → analysis → reveal**

Favor questions involving money, value, taxes, remodeling, land, neighborhood effects, property characteristics, comparability, or decisions homeowners actually make.

## Washington homeowner concern pillars

### Assessment changes

Typical concerns:

- value changed even though the property did not
- no recent physical inspection
- neighborhood-wide changes
- land versus improvement movement
- sold versus unsold treatment
- statistical/mass-appraisal behavior

Strong investigations compare cohorts, neighborhoods, sold/unsold properties, permit/no-permit groups, land/improvement components, or outlier changes.

### Assessed value versus property tax

Look for divergence rather than only definitions:

- assessment up / tax down
- assessment down / tax up
- same value / different tax result
- large assessment change / modest tax change

Verify current Washington levy/tax rules with authoritative sources each time.

### Remodels and improvements

Questions include:

- remodel cost versus observed market contribution
- roof/maintenance versus physical improvement
- addition or finished area
- garage or shop
- actual year versus effective year
- permit activity and subsequent assessed/market behavior

Never assume cost equals contributory value.

### ADUs and secondary dwellings

Investigate:

- ADU versus non-ADU market behavior
- detached versus attached where data supports it
- ADUs on small versus larger sites
- garage conversion versus new detached structure where observable
- assessment behavior and current Washington rules

### Land and acreage

Investigate:

- marginal acreage contribution
- acreage bands
- where additional acreage appears to flatten
- utilities/access/zoning/waterfront/site constraints where authoritative data exists
- acreage interactions with dwelling size, shops, or location

Do not use naive price-per-acre multiplication as a valuation conclusion.

### Land versus improvements

Look for component divergence:

- land up / improvement down
- land down / improvement up
- changing land share of total value
- old structures on increasingly valuable sites

### Comparable properties

Use “same X, different Y” stories around:

- size
- acreage
- age/effective age
- condition/quality
- garage/shop
- waterfront/view
- utilities
- neighborhood
- sale date

### Neighborhood effects

Investigate:

- adjacent neighborhoods
- appraisal-area boundaries
- city/unincorporated
- subdivision/rural
- waterfront/inland
- differential market change over time

Maps are especially valuable when the geography itself is part of the evidence.

### Property-record accuracy

Questions include:

- wrong living area
- wrong finished area
- missing/incorrect garage or outbuilding
- year/effective year discrepancy
- condition disagreement
- permit record versus property record

Do not imply every record discrepancy changes value materially.

### Appeals

Explore what evidence actually supports a market-value disagreement. Distinguish percentage change, neighbor comparisons, automated estimates, market sales, record errors, physical-condition evidence, and development constraints.

Stay neutral and verify current appeal rules.

### Market behavior

Prefer specific relationships over generic market updates:

- assessment-to-sale ratios
- cohort performance
- neighborhood divergence
- property type/characteristic differences
- turning markets

## Question engines

### Same X, Different Y

Hold a major characteristic approximately constant and vary another.

### More Is Not Necessarily Linear

Test marginal contribution and possible thresholds/plateaus.

### Variables People Assume Move Together

Examples: assessment/tax, acreage/price, remodel cost/value, land/improvement, house size/price.

### Nothing Changed, But the Number Changed

Useful for mass appraisal, neighborhood repricing, and component allocation.

### Something Changed, But Buyers Barely Reacted

Useful for additions, acreage, shops, garages, remodels, and other features.

### Boundary Effect

Compare across meaningful geographic or administrative boundaries.

### Which Matters More?

Compare competing characteristics while controlling obvious confounders.

### Outliers

Find unusual changes, ratios, land shares, or sale outcomes; first rule out bad data.

### Before and After

Use for permits, remodels, assessment cycles, or market periods. Timing alone does not prove causation.

### Expected vs. Observed

Start with a common homeowner belief and test it without committing to the outcome.

## Investigation methods

Use the simplest credible method:

- cohort comparison
- matched properties
- binned analysis
- time series
- boundary comparison
- ratio analysis
- multivariable analysis when needed

Prefer medians when outliers can distort averages. Use regression only when it materially improves the evidence.

## Evidence language

Prefer:

- “in this sample”
- “associated with”
- “showed a higher median”
- “remained related after accounting for...”

Avoid fixed contributory-value claims unless the evidence genuinely supports them.

## Discovery scoring

Score 0–5:

- homeowner relevance
- surprise potential
- testability
- generalizability
- evidence strength
- visual potential
- novelty

Maximum 35.

Guideline:

- 30–35: strong production candidate
- 26–29: strong investigation candidate
- 22–25: investigate selectively
- under 22: usually reject

Apply penalties for tiny samples, weak controls, stale/unreliable data, duplication, parcel-centric framing, privacy risk, predetermined conclusions, or low visual/story value.

## Weak-topic rejection

Usually reject definition-only topics such as:

- What is assessed value?
- What is a comparable?
- How does property tax work?
- What is an acre?

Convert them into unresolved investigations.

## Source hierarchy

Use community sources to discover demand/questions. Use authoritative sources for rules. Use structured property data to test market/property hypotheses.

For current Washington rules, do fresh verification rather than relying on static examples in this guide.

## Core test

A strong investigation should answer yes to most:

- Would a homeowner care immediately?
- Is the answer uncertain?
- Can available data test it?
- Is there a competing explanation?
- Is it broader than one parcel?
- Can it be shown visually?
- Would either outcome be interesting?
